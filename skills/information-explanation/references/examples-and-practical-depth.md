# Examples and practical depth

Use this when an accurate explanation still leaves a missing bridge, or an example risks changing the model. Add detail only when it resolves that gap. The evidence contract controls which facts, illustrations, and claims may be introduced.

## Identify the missing bridge

| Reader difficulty | Possible addition |
|---|---|
| Cannot recognize the concept in a situation | A short case with observable cues |
| Confuses it with familiar advice or a nearby concept | A contrast on the defining difference |
| Understands the rule but misapplies it | A relevant exception, boundary, or overlooked condition |
| Understands advice but cannot follow it | The actual obstacle and a feasible route, if practical action is in scope |
| Does not see why the obstacle changes the result | The missing mechanism or dependency |

These are choices, not steps every answer must complete. Orientation can explain why a mechanism matters without becoming an implementation guide. When action is promised, however, naming a target state alone may leave the task unfinished.

Hypothetical editing example: “Group related material” becomes recognizable with “If several paragraphs answer ‘What can block approval?’ but are scattered among background sections, collect them under the approval conditions.” A useful boundary is “Keep an incident's chronological evidence together when moving it would obscure the sequence.” This supplies a cue and a limit, not a paragraph quota.

## Control what an example implies

An example can illustrate a relation, represent typical cases, enumerate all permitted cases, or establish a binding contract. Check which role the text actually assigns it. Do not let an illustration become an exhaustive rule or an advertised guarantee through missing scope.

Hypothetical system: a saved report references a field, and removing that field makes the report invalid. This shows how a change can invalidate a saved view through a dependency. It does not establish that every change invalidates every report. Keep the abstraction and identify the dependency rather than replacing the concept with one story.

Label invented details as hypothetical where they could be mistaken for observations or evidence. A vivid scenario must not imply measured performance, real customer experience, or confirmed product behavior. Source examples can explain a teaching method without validating the domain claims in that example.

## Remove distractions while retaining the mechanism

Choose details that reveal the relevant relation. Names, dates, occupations, and dramatic stakes can attract objections unrelated to it; omit them unless they carry a necessary condition. Do not strip the condition that makes an example work merely to shorten it.

Reuse a domain across nearby examples when it supports transfer. Change domains when another reveals a limitation or better matches the reader's experience, and explain the connection. There is no fixed example count or abstract-to-concrete ratio.

Keep useful terminology. A recognizable case can explain a term while letting that term support later reasoning. An analogy should preserve the relevant structure and say where it stops applying; surface resemblance does not justify extending its conclusions.

## Investigate practical barriers

When apparently obvious advice is not followed, ask what prevents it: an unavailable input, competing constraint, uncertainty about recognition, or disrupted sequence. Use known barriers; do not infer laziness or ignorance. Useful follow-ups are how the reader would recognize the condition and what changes when it does not hold.

If the promised route depends on technical or other domain facts, use verified task material or the appropriate evidence workflow. If those facts are absent, identify the missing bridge instead of inventing an authoritative procedure. Technical and execution artifacts keep their owners and verification conditions.
